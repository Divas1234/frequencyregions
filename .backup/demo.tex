\begin{abstract}
Ensuring frequency dynamic security with considering uncertain variability (UV) is a critical challenge in low-inertia grids with a high penetration of renewable energy sources. In this situation, a Bayesian-filer-derived system frequency response (BF-SFR) method and a well-designed chance constrained frequency dynamic unit commitment (CC-FDUC) model are proposed to solve this problem. A Bayesian filter method for modeling SFR is created after the UV induced by numerous converter-interfaced resources and potential measured errors is examined. Unlike previous approaches such as analytical SFR method and time-discretized ones without considering UV, BF-SFR offers the trajectory of frequency derivation as existing methods do, it additionally estimate the posterior distribution of frequency derivation if UV incorporated. Following this, The posterior distribution through BF-SFR is reformulated as a set of chance constrained frequency dynamic restrictions in CC-FDUC to search optimal scheduled commitment results. Several cases on a modified IEEE 6 bus test system and a 118 bus test system confirm the unbiased and validness of the proposed models.
\end{abstract}

Bayesian filer, system frequency response, uncertain variability, unit commitment, chance constraints, converter-interfaced generators.
\end{IEEEkeywords}


\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} UV characteristic from multiple converter-interfaced generators and measured errors has a negative impact on the frequency dynamic security and energy management in low-inertia grids. To keep the system have an adequate capacity against UV, many studies and state Grid Codes are reported to explore the necessity of grid-connected converter-interfaced generators participating into frequency supporting in order to preserve the intended system's acceptable capacity against UV.

The literature reported several studies to model SFR against imbalanced power disturbances. These studies used an center-of-inertia (CoI) swing equation to calculate frequency dynamics. Ref. $[1,2]$ built a low-order CoI equation concerning conventional synchronous generators and derived an analytical solution. Ref. [3-6] considered the impact of converter-interfaced generators with various frequency control schemes. It's worth noting that such models in [1-6] kept similar mathematical types of ordinary differential equation. As such, their solutions kept similar forms, the difference of associated solutions lies in a modification of frequency dynamic parameters due to integrated converter-interfaced generators. The shortage of such solutions mentioned before lies in a lack of consideration of complex frequency control schemes. Driven by this issue, another type of time-discretized solution encoding SFR is developed. Ref. $[7,8]$ constructed a time-discretized solution considering rate-of-change-of-frequency (RoCoF) replays. Ref. [9] extended a multi-area time-discretized solution considering frequency supporting of fly-wheel storage systems. Ref. [11] derived a time-discretized SFR solution considering converter-based generators. The numerical result of frequency response, [7-11], reflects the trajectory of frequency dynamic with free noise assumptions. Nevertheless, the frequency dynamic with noise assumptions cannot be solved. Furthermore, the probabilistic distribution of the system frequency change cannot be well estimated.

In the application of frequency dynamic in electrical power system operations, some FDUC models were carried out on introducing frequency dynamic requirements derived from SFR into UC problems. These models can be divided into two categories in line with integrated frequency dynamic constraints. i) critical feature requirement of frequency dynamic, such as RoCoF, nadir, is reshaped as a set of convex constraints into UC $[3,5,6$, 11-17]; ii) frequency change over a predefined simulated horizon (e.g., 0s - 60s) is reformulated into a set of binding constraints incorporating into UC, [7, 9, 18-19]. Compared with the former category, the latter one is more accurate but more complicated. In despite that the involvement of conventional generators participating in UC has been thoroughly discussed, few studies focus frequency supporting of converter-interfaced generators and BESS from the probabilistic perspective.

Motivated by such issues, this paper firstly presents a new probabilistic Bayesian filter framework for encoding SFR with UV hypothesis. BF-SFR captures the interactive impact of UV on the frequency derivation, it can not only estimate the trajectory of frequency derivation but also provide its posterior probabilistic density distribution (PDF). Therefore, BF-SFR provides a great level of freedom for analyzing frequency dynamic and external factors such as UV, unit's output limits, multiple control interaction. Following this, a classical application, FDUC is implemented. In particular, the impact of frequency dynamic requirement with UV assumption within BF-SFR is extended, and chance-constrained FDUC model is well redesigned.

In all, the contributions in this paper can be summarized,

1) A new BF-SFR solution for modeling SFR is proposed. The impact of frequency dynamic with UV assumption is investigated. By implementing it, the probabilistic distribution of frequency derivation can be well estimated. Meanwhile, the effect of various control schemes provided by converter-interfaced generators and BESS can be smoothly reconsidered.

2) A new CCFDUC model with considering frequency dynamic derived from BF-SFR is designed. A chance constraint form of frequency dynamic requirement concerning nadir is derived. The convex characteristic and linearization of constructed frequency dynamic constraint is discussed.

The reminder of this paper is organized: Section II introduces a Bayesian filter framework for encoding SFR and derives the solution of BF-SFR. Section III constructs a set of chance-constraint frequency dynamic constraints to enforce the impact of frequency dynamic requirements, and sequentially gives a CCFDUC model. Section IV verifies the effectiveness of BF-SFR and CCFDUC in a modified IEEE 14 bus test system and IEEE 118 bus test system, and Section V concludes on the main work in this paper.

Considering a low-inertia grid comprised of conventional generators, converter-interfaced generators and BESS, the union frequency dynamic of studied power system can be combined as Fig. \ref{fig1}(a) and simplified as Fig. \ref{fig1}(b). The impact of homogeneous generators in Fig. \ref{fig1}(a) with similar frequency control schemes can be equivalent as an aggregated $\mathrm{CoI}$ unit, illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig1}(b). As such, the aggregated coefficients $\left\{H_{G}, D_{G}, K_{G}, F_{G}, R_{G}, T_{G}\right\},\left\{K_{W}, R_{W}, M_{W}, D_{W}, T_{W}\right\}$ and $\left\{T_{E}, R_{E}\right\}$, respectively, represent the adjusted output produced by $\mathrm{CoI}$ conventional unit block, $\mathrm{CoI}$ converter-interfaced block unit and CoI BESS block. More details on the calculation of aggregated frequency parameters can be found in $[6]$.

In Fig. \ref{fig1}(b), the reduced order transfer function of SFR concerning a step-type mismatch power disturbance $\Delta p_{{step }}(s)=\Delta p_{{step }} / s$ can be rewritten as,

\begin{align}\label{eqn:1}
& \left(M_{G} s+D_{G}\right) \Delta f(s)=\left(\frac{1}{s} \Delta p_{ {step }}\right)- \\
& \qquad\underbrace{\left(\frac{K_{G}}{ R_{G}}\right)\left[\frac{\left(1+F_{G} T_{G} s\right)}{\left(1+T_{G} s\right)}\right] \Delta f(s)}_{\text {CoI conventional unit }}- \notag\\
& \qquad\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{R_{W}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+T_{W} s}\right) \Delta f(s)}_{\text {CoI Droop-controlled converter-interfaced unit }}-\underbrace{\left(M_{E} s+D_{E}\right) \Delta f(s)}_{\text {CoI BESS }} \notag
\end{align}

The $t$ - domain mathematical form of \eqref{eqn:1} can be transferred by utilizing the inverse Laplace transformation,

\begin{subequations}\label{eqn:2}
\begin{align}
& M_{e q u} \Delta f^{\prime}(t)+D_{e q u} \Delta f(t)=\Delta p(t) \\
& \Delta p(t)=\Delta p_{\text {step }}-\Delta p_{a d d}^{G}(t)-\Delta p_{a d d}^{W}(t)-\Delta p_{a d d}^{E}(t) \\
& M_{e q u}=D_{G}+D_{W} \\
& D_{\text {equ }}=D_{G}+D_{w}+\frac{1}{ R_{W}} +\frac{1}{ R_{E}} \\
& \Delta p_{\text {add }}^{G}(t)=\frac{\kappa_{G} F_{G}}{ R_{G}} \Delta f(t)+\frac{1}{ T_{G} }\biggl\{ \\
& \quad \int_{0}^{t}\left[\left(D_{e q u}+\frac{K_{G}}{ R_{G}}\right) \Delta f(t)-\Delta p_{\text {step }}\right] d t+M_{g} \Delta f(t)\biggr\} \notag\\
& \Delta p_{a d d}^{W}(t)=\left(\frac{1}{R_{W}}\right) \Delta f(t)\\
& \Delta p_{a d d}^{E}(t)=\left(\frac{1}{ R_{E}}\right) \Delta f(t)
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Detailed derivation on \eqref{eqn:1} $\rightarrow$ \eqref{eqn:2} can be found at appendix II. By replacing $\Delta f^{\prime}(t)|_{t = t_0 + n \Delta t}$ as its equivalent discretized form $\sfrac{[\Delta f(t_0+ (n+1)\Delta t)-\Delta f(t_0 + n\Delta t)]}{ \Delta t}$, the time-discretized form of $\Delta f(t)$ can be well estimated as below,

\begin{subequations}\label{eqn:3}
\begin{align}
& \Delta f(t_0 +(n + 1)\Delta t)=\Delta f(t_0 + n \Delta t)+\\
& \quad\qquad \frac{1}{M_{e q u}}\bigl[\Delta p(t_0 + n \Delta t)-D_{e q u} \Delta f(t_0 + n \Delta t)\bigr] \label{time-discretized SFR: swing equation}\\
& \Delta p(t_0 + n \Delta t)=\Delta p_{{step }}-\Delta p_{{add }}^{G}(t_0 + n \Delta t)-\label{time-discretized SFR: mismatch power}\\
&\quad\qquad\Delta p_{{add }}^{W}(t_0 + n \Delta t)-\Delta p_{\text {add }}^{E}(t_0 + n \Delta t) \notag\\
&\Delta p_{a d d}^{G}(t_0 + n \Delta t)=\frac{\kappa_{G} F_{G}}{ R_{G} }\Delta f(t_0 + n\Delta t)+\label{time-discretized SFR: additional power from convertional units}\\
& \quad\qquad \frac{1}{T_{G}}\biggl\{M_{g} \Delta f(t_0 + n\Delta t)+ \notag\\
& \quad\qquad \sum_{n=0}^{N T}\left[\left(D_{e q u}+\frac{K_{G}}{ R_{G}}\right) \Delta f\left(t_{0}+n \Delta t\right)-\Delta p_{ {step }}\right] \Delta t\biggr\} \notag\\
& \Delta p_{\text {add }}^{W}(t_0+n\Delta t)=\left(\frac{1}{ R_{W} }\right) \Delta f(t_0+n\Delta t) \label{time-discretized SFR: additional power from converters}\\
& \Delta p_{\text {add }}^{E}(t_0+n\Delta t)=\left(\frac{1} { R_{E} }\right) \Delta f(t_0+n\Delta t) \label{additional power from bess}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent where $N T$ is a total number of simulated steps, $\Delta t$ declares a simulated discretized step (in this paper, $\Delta t=0.05 s$ ). \eqref{time-discretized SFR: swing equation} depicts the temporal evolution of frequency derivation between adjacent discretized steps; \eqref{time-discretized SFR: mismatch power} describes the derivation of mismatch power injected into power systems; \eqref{time-discretized SFR: additional power from convertional units}, \eqref{time-discretized SFR: additional power from converters} and \eqref{additional power from bess}  represent additional regulated outputs produced by different grid-connected units. Particularly, a state transfer model concerning SFR can be actually observed, extracted and illustrated as (3) from \eqref{time-discretized SFR: swing equation} - \eqref{time-discretized SFR: mismatch power}.

% Since $\Delta p_{a d d}^{G}, \Delta p_{a d d}^{W}$ and $\Delta p_{a d d}^{E}$ are independent on aggregated frequency dynamic parameters and previous frequency derivation.
Let $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t_0 +n\Delta t), \mu_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ represents the posterior PDF of $\Delta f(t_0 +n \Delta t)$ at the $n-th$ discretized step; $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta p(t_0 +n \Delta t), \mu_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right)$ represents the posterior PDF of $\Delta p(t_0 +n \Delta t)$ at the $n-th$ discretized step. $\left\{\mu_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right\}$ and $\left\{\mu_{1}, \sigma_{2}\right\}$, respectively, describes associated characterized value (e.g., expectation and standard variance) representing residuals in observation process and measured process. Then, $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t_0 +n \Delta t), \mu_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ and $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta p(t_0 +n \Delta t), \mu_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right)$ are capable to reflect the impact of UV feature on the frequency changing process. Furthermore, a Bayesian-based BF framework is implemented to depict the interactive relation between $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t_0 + n\Delta t), \mu_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ and $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta p(t_0 +n \Delta t), \mu_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right)$.

In \eqref{eqn:4}, $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t+\Delta t\right), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ denotes an equivalent mathematical form of $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$. It depicts that the posterior PDF of $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ can be fully inferred after obtaining all measured sequences $\Delta p\left(t_{0}: t+\Delta t\right)=\left\{\Delta p_{t} \mid t \in\left[t_{0}, t+\Delta t\right]\right\}$. Moreover, the calculation of $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t+\Delta t\right), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ can be indirectly achieved by estimating its prior form $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t\right), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)$ and a likelihood rate $\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta p(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t\right), u_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right)$. The below gives the expression of the prior function and the likelihood ratio.

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t\right), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right)=\int \mathbb{P}(\Delta f(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta f(t), \\
\left.u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right) \mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t\right), u_{1}, \sigma_{1}\right) d \Delta f(t) \\
\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta p(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t\right), u_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right)=\int \mathbb{P}(\Delta p(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta f(t+\Delta t) \\
\left.u_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right) \mathbb{P}\left(\Delta f(t+\Delta t) \mid \Delta p\left(t_{0}: t\right), u_{2}, \sigma_{2}\right) d \Delta p(t)
\end{gathered}
$$

In this work, UV is abstracted as a sequence of residuals series, and it reflects: 1) the intermittent property of converter-interfaced generators participating in frequency supporting, and 2) possible measured errors of $\Delta f(t)$ at each discretized step. \section{Mathematical Typography \\ and Why It Matters}

\noindent Typographical conventions for mathematical formulas have been developed to {\bf provide uniformity and clarity of presentation across mathematical texts}. This enables the readers of those texts to both understand the author's ideas and to grasp new concepts quickly. While software such as \LaTeX \ and MathType\textsuperscript{\textregistered} can produce aesthetically pleasing math when used properly, it is also very easy to misuse the software, potentially resulting in incorrect math display.

IEEE aims to provide authors with the proper guidance on mathematical typesetting style and assist them in writing the best possible article.

As such, IEEE has assembled a set of examples of good and bad mathematical typesetting. You will see how various issues are dealt with. The following publications have been referenced in preparing this material:

\begin{list}{}{}
\item{\emph{Mathematics into Type}, published by the American Mathematical Society}
\item{\emph{The Printing of Mathematics}, published by Oxford University Press}
\item{\emph{The \LaTeX Companion}, by F. Mittelbach and M. Goossens}
\item{\emph{More Math into LaTeX}, by G. Gr\"atzer}
\item{AMS-StyleGuide-online.pdf, published by the American Mathematical Society}
\end{list}

Further examples can be seen at \url{http://journals.ieeeauthorcenter.ieee.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/IEEE-Math-Typesetting-Guide.pdf}

\subsection{Equation Numbering}
\noindent {\bf{Consecutive Numbering:}} Equations within an article are numbered consecutively from the beginning of the
article to the end, i.e., (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), etc. Do not use roman numerals or section numbers for equation numbering.\\

\noindent {\bf{Appendix Equations:}} The continuation of consecutively numbered equations is best in the Appendix, but numbering
 as (A1), (A2), etc., is permissible.\\

\noindent {\bf{Hyphens and Periods}}: Hyphens and periods should not be used in equation numbers, i.e., use (1a) rather than
(1-a) and (2a) rather than (2.a) for sub-equations. This should be consistent throughout the article.

\subsection{Cases Structures}
\noindent Many times we find cases coded using the wrong environment, i.e., {\tt{array}}. Using the {\tt{cases}} environment will save keystrokes (from not having to type the $\backslash${\tt{left}}$\backslash${\tt{lbrace}}) and automatically provide the correct column alignment.
\begin{equation*}
{z_m(t)} = \begin{cases}
1,&{\text{if}}\ {\beta }_m(t) \\
{0,}&{\text{otherwise.}}
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\noindent is coded as follows:
\begin{verbatim}
\begin{equation*}
{z_m(t)} =
\begin{cases}
1,&{\text{if}}\ {\beta }_m(t),\\
{0,}&{\text{otherwise.}}
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\end{verbatim}
\noindent Note that the ``\&'' is used to mark the tabular alignment. This is important to get  proper column alignment. Do not use $\backslash${\tt{quad}} or other fixed spaces to try and align the columns. Also, note the use of the $\backslash${\tt{text}} macro for text elements such as ``if'' and ``otherwise''.

\noindent Some improper mathematical coding advice has been given in various YouTube\textsuperscript{TM} videos on how to write scholarly articles, so please follow these good examples:\\

\section{Additional Advice}

Please use ``soft'' (e.g., \verb|\eqref{Eq}|) or \verb|(\ref{Eq})|
cross references instead of ``hard'' references (e.g., \verb|(1)|).
That will make it possible to combine sections, add equations, or
change the order of figures or citations without having to go through
the file line by line.

Please note that the \verb|{subequations}| environment in {\LaTeX}
will increment the main equation counter even when there are no
equation numbers displayed. If you forget that, you might write an
article in which the equation numbers skip from (17) to (20), causing
the copy editors to wonder if you've discovered a new method of
counting.

{\BibTeX} does not work by magic. It doesn't get the bibliographic
data from thin air but from .bib files. If you use {\BibTeX} to produce a
bibliography you must send the .bib files.

{\LaTeX} can't read your mind. If you assign the same label to a
subsubsection and a table, you might find that Table I has been cross
referenced as Table IV-B3.

{\LaTeX} does not have precognitive abilities. If you put a
\verb|\label| command before the command that updates the counter it's
supposed to be using, the label will pick up the last counter to be
cross referenced instead. In particular, a \verb|\label| command
should not go before the caption of a figure or a table.

Please do not use \verb|\nonumber| or \verb|\notag| inside the
\verb|{array}| environment. It will not stop equation numbers inside
\verb|{array}| (there won't be any anyway) and it might stop a wanted
equation number in the surrounding equation.
